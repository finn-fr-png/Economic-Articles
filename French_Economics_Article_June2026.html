Can the European Union Grow Without Becoming a Political Union?

The EU single market made the continent richer. Keeping it growing may now require the one thing voters have been most reluctant to give: real political power.

By Finnigan French

For most of its history, the European project has asked very little of its citizens. It asked them to give up tariffs, accept cross-border competition, in some cases give up monetary sovereignty, and trust that the gains would follow. Mostly they did, and mostly the gains did follow. However, the economic growth that once came easily has slowed and many countries are sinking deeper into stagflation. The steps needed to maintain the prosperity that EU citizens have become accustomed to requires something much harder to sell: pooling the power to tax, to spend and to defend on a supranational level.

A slowing engine

Let's start with the numbers. Eurostat's first estimate put EU economic growth at 1.0% in 2024, up from 0.4% the year before, with the euro area at 0.9%.1 Although this is a recovery, it is a modest one. Across the Atlantic, the US economy grew 2.8% in 2024, nearly three times the EU's pace.2 The gap between EU and US growth has become a fixture of European policy debate.

The gillets-jaune protests in France which produced some of the most intense rioting in year as well cost of living protests in Spain highlight the growing discontent with stagflation. Economists tend to blame the usual suspects: fragmented markets, weak productivity, expensive energy since the war in Ukraine and an ageing workforce. All of those matter but what to do about them remains controversial.

A currency without a safety net

Economists have already identified what goes wrong when a group of countries shares a currency but not much else. In his 1961 theory of optimum currency areas, Robert Mundell pointed out that a country inside such a union can no longer devalue or set its own interest rates to escape a local slump. It needs another shock absorber, in a federation that is usually the central budget. When a region of the United States falls into recession, federal taxes and transfers cushion it automatically.

The European Union has no equivalent. Its budget for 2021–2027 is about 1.02% of EU gross national income.9 That is enough for farm subsidies and regional programmes but it is nowhere near enough to absorb a crisis. The euro's early years showed the possible consequences of the absence of a sufficient fiscal transfers. Before 2008, money flowed from the north of the continent to the south as cheap credit. When that flow stopped, Greece, Ireland, Spain and Portugal faced sovereign debt crises, and the burden fell on austerity rather than shared support.

More recently, Europe responded to the Covid-19 pandemic economic recession with the pandemic recovery fund which was truly an innovation as it represents the first instance of EU common borrowing. But "temporary" is the operative word. A permanent fiscal union would mean deciding together how common money is spent and who ultimately pays it back. But are countries willing to cede sovereignty in this way?

In the meantime, the European Central Bank has carried much of the load. It held interest rates at historic lows and bought bonds on an unprecedented scale. That kept the currency together, but monetary policy cannot build a rail network, fund a defence industry or insure the unemployed. Its tools have limits, and years of ultra-low rates carry risks of their own.

A shared fiscal policy would give the euro area the shock absorber it currently lacks. A common fiscal framework could work the way US federal taxes and transfers do: money flows automatically to regions hit hardest by a slump, and the stabilisation happens in months rather than through years of austerity. It could also fund investment that heavily indebted member states cannot finance alone, such as cross-border digital infrastructure, green energy and research, and jointly issued Eurobonds could lower borrowing costs for everyone. In practice, this would mean a permanent central budget with real spending power, possibly backed by common debt, and a shared unemployment insurance scheme that keeps workers moving across borders when one economy weakens. Because it would also mean deciding together how money is raised, spent and repaid, it would need democratic accountability to be politically sustainable. But does the political will for such a cessession of sovereignty exist in a EU plagued by rising nationalist populism?

Monetary policy can hold a currency together but it cannot build the shared fiscal infrastructure an economic union needs to deal with crises.

Defence is the other bill

The second argument is about defence, where the EU is completely fragmented. Russia's invasion of Ukraine pushed European governments to spend more. EU countries spent €343 billion on defence in 2024, against €845 billion in the United States, according to figures reported by Euronews from the European Defence Agency.8

But was this money spent efficiently? EU members field seventeen different types of main battle tank, while the United States fields one.5 Because of this, Ukraine needs ten types of howitzer meant ten supply chains to keep running.6 In the same way, the research budget is small and scattered. Member states spent about €10.7 billion on defence research in 2022, roughly $11.8 billion, against $140 billion in the United States.3 Although Europe's figure has grown since, with the European Defence Agency counting €13 billion in 2024.4, the gap between the EU and the US is still measured in multiples.

The result is a continent that pays more for less. Common procurement and a common budget would let European governments buy in bulk and back a handful of firms able to compete at scale. Without them, money gets spread across national champions, each with its own designs and suppliers. Pooling procurement means pooling the decision about what to buy and from whom.

The legitimacy gap

The political scientist Fritz Scharpf offered a useful distinction. The European Union, he argued, had long drawn its legitimacy from output: it delivered prosperity and stability, so voters did not need to feel they had chosen it. Scharpf contrasted that with input legitimacy, the sense that citizens shape decisions through elections and public debate. For decades the first carried the second.

That bargain is fraying. The rise of Eurosceptic parties and the Brexit referendum both show citizens distrusting decisions made by institutions they cannot directly elect or remove. The people who would have to finance a fiscal union, or fund a shared army, need to believe the institutions making those choices answer to them. Without that belief, the other two forms of integration stay blocked.

What would change this? The proposals are concrete. The European Parliament, the only directly elected EU body, could be given the right to initiate legislation rather than mostly react to what the Commission proposes. Decisions on taxation and foreign policy could move from unanimity to qualified majority voting, so that a single government could no longer hold the whole bloc hostage, as Hungary has repeatedly shown it can. Part of the Parliament could be elected from pan-European lists, which would encourage transnational parties instead of national lists summed together. And a finance minister accountable to Parliament could give a future fiscal union a democratic face.

Some evidence suggests the public is more open than the politics implies. Support for EU action on defence and energy security has been high in opinion polls, which hints that discontent is less about what the EU does than about how it is governed.

Muddling through

The honest conclusion is that the EU is unlikely to take this path soon. Northern voters are unwilling to finance a transfer union. Southern voters are unwilling to accept the conditions northern governments would demand in return. Eurosceptic parties are in government in some member states and polling strongly in others, which has changed how cautious mainstream leaders behave. Russia's war and a more distant American partner have created real urgency, but not yet the kind of shock that forced past leaps, such as the postwar coal and steel community or the reunification of Germany that made the Maastricht Treaty possible.

What Europe has built instead is incremental crisis management: the bond-buying programme, the pandemic recovery fund and the rearmament plans are each meaningful, and each is a response to an emergency rather than a step toward a common state. Europe's leaders have fashioned a fallback that keeps the currency and the single market intact. The European Commission's Readiness 2030 plan, for example, aims to unlock up to €800 billion in fiscal space over four years for defence.8 Yet it stops well short of the common borrowing, common procurement and common democratic accountability that a fully functioning economic union would require.

Unless a severe shock, such as a sovereign default, a war on EU territory or a decisive American withdrawal from NATO, forces the issue, the likely outcome is a managed, lower-growth equilibrium. Europe will remain more stable than its sceptics predict and less prosperous than it could be. The single market made Europe rich in an era when the bargain was easy. Keeping it rich may require a harder conversation about who decides, and how those decisions can be made legitimate in the eyes of the people who must live with them.
