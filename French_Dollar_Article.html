Is the Dollar Holding Back the Developing World?

It prices much of the world's oil, turns up in almost every currency trade and fills central banks' vaults. For poorer countries, it is both a safe harbour and a straitjacket.

By Finnigan French
The United States Federal Reserve building in Washington, DC.

In 2018, the Argentine peso lost more than half its value against the US dollar.1 That would hurt any country. For Argentina it was worse, because much of its borrowing was in dollars, so every peso lost made the debt heavier in local terms.1 By the autumn the government was leaning on an IMF programme that had grown to $57.1 billion, the largest stand-by arrangement in the Fund's history.2

Argentina is an extreme case, but not an isolated one. It points to a question economists have argued over for decades: when almost the whole world prices, borrows and saves in one country's money, who carries the risk? This article argues that the dollar's dominance does act as a brake on growth in developing economies, through trade and through debt, but that the alternatives are not yet ready to take its place.

01

The currency of everything

The dollar's rise began at Bretton Woods in 1944, when other currencies were pegged to the dollar and the dollar was tied to gold. The gold link was cut in the early 1970s, but the dollar's status survived. It had acquired something stickier than a legal guarantee: habit.

The numbers are striking. In April 2025 the dollar was on one side of 89% of all foreign-exchange trades,3 a share that has barely moved in about 25 years even as the market has grown enormously.4

Central banks tell the same story. According to the IMF's latest figures, 56.7% of the world's foreign-exchange reserves are held in dollars and 20.6% in euros. Yen, sterling, renminbi and everything else share the remaining 22.7%.7

Meanwhile, the countries that depend on this system have become the larger part of the world economy. Emerging and developing economies now account for almost 60% of global GDP on a purchasing-power basis, up from 37% in the 1980s,8 yet their currencies are rarely used to settle cross-border deals or held in reserve. Defenders of the system point to its stability, its deep markets and the investment it attracts. The rest of this article asks what it costs.

02

When a cheaper currency doesn't buy more exports

Introductory economics offers a comforting rule. If a country's currency falls, its goods become cheaper abroad, foreigners buy more of them, and exports pull the economy out of trouble.

In a dollar-priced world, that chain can break at the first link. Roughly 40% of world goods trade is invoiced in dollars,9 and for Asian and Latin American emerging economies the dollar is the dominant invoicing currency, with shares that have been remarkably stable for two decades.10 When an exporter prices in dollars, a falling local currency barely changes what the foreign buyer pays: the dollar price stays put, so the buyer has no new reason to order more.10 Imports, meanwhile, are also priced in dollars and become dearer in local terms. Adjustment ends up working mainly through imports, with households and firms buying less, rather than through exports.10

Brazil offers a flavour of the problem. In 2016 the real was worth about 38% less against the dollar than its 2013 average.11 Yet by several measures that adjust for prices and for Brazil's trading partners' currencies, it had barely moved from its 2013 level, which shows that a headline fall against the dollar is not the same as a gain in competitiveness.11

The consequences reach beyond trade. If a currency cannot reliably win back competitiveness, a factory in a poorer country becomes a less certain bet for a foreign investor. That may help explain what economists call Lucas's paradox: capital does not flow from rich countries to poor ones as readily as theory predicts. The link is plausible, not proven. Nor should the dollar take all the blame. The troubles above also reflect political instability, weak commodity prices and fragile public finances at home, and blaming the dollar is politically convenient.

03

Debts you cannot print

The second mechanism is debt. Poorer governments often have to borrow in foreign currency, because lenders abroad prefer it. Most of what African governments owe to China, for example, is denominated in dollars, and the IMF sees no clear downward trend in that share.12

The trouble starts when the local currency falls. A debt of a billion dollars is still a billion dollars, but it takes more pesos, cedis or naira to repay it. A central bank can print its own currency to calm a domestic crisis; it cannot print dollars. If it tries to defend its currency by raising interest rates instead, it risks choking the very economy it is trying to protect. As money leaves, there is less left to spend on roads, hospitals and schools.

Argentina in 2018 shows the loop: the peso falls, the dollar debt grows heavier, confidence drops and the peso falls again.1

The IMF's own post-mortem is blunt. It concluded that the programme did not achieve its aim of restoring confidence in Argentina's finances while fostering growth.2 The dollar was not the only culprit, of course. A severe drought and an overvalued peso also played their part.1 But the episode is a warning about what happens when a country owes money in a currency it cannot print.

04

The least bad option

If the dollar is so costly, why not leave? Because for any single country, leaving is costlier still.

Money is a little like a language. Its value lies in how many other people already use it. English is not the world's most elegant language, but it is the one that business partners are most likely to share. The dollar works the same way. It is backed by the world's largest economy and by the deepest financial markets, and US Treasury bonds are widely treated as the safest asset there is. That is why the dollar's share of currency trading has hardly moved in a quarter of a century.4 The more people use it, the harder it is to do without it.

For an exporter in a developing country, there is also a practical point. Importers want a price they can rely on, and many local currencies cannot offer that. Switching to local currency invoicing could cost an exporter customers, and a government that tries to force the change risks scaring off the foreign capital it needs.

05

Cracks, or just a wobble?

2025 gave sceptics something to point to. The US dollar index fell 10.8% in the first half of the year, its worst first half since 1973, as investors reconsidered their appetite for US assets.13

Yet in the reserves data the shift is far more modest than the headlines suggest. IMF economists found that most of the apparent dip in the dollar's share in the second quarter of 2025 came from exchange-rate movements, with the euro and other currencies rising against the dollar, rather than from central banks selling dollars.14 Across the five quarters from the second quarter of 2025 to the second quarter of 2026, the share has stayed between 56.4% and 57.2%.

The main challenger is China. The renminbi now takes part in 8.5% of currency trades,6 but it makes up under 2% of reserves.16 Beijing's central bank had signed 40 bilateral swap agreements with central banks around the world by 2021,12 and at the 2024 China–Africa forum, China for the first time denominated its financing commitments in renminbi.17 Proposals for a joint BRICS currency and for central-bank digital currencies keep the idea of an alternative alive. Both face a basic obstacle: a currency is only as useful as the markets and trust behind it, and those take decades to build.

So is the dollar a brake on developing countries? In two specific ways, the evidence says yes: it dulls the benefit of a weaker currency, and it turns every currency slide into a debt problem. But it is also the least bad option available today, and its hold is strengthened by the very habit of using it.

If the dollar's dominance fades, it will probably fade slowly. The greater risk may be a disorderly transition, because the countries with the thinnest financial buffers would feel the shock first.
